17:T82b1,<h2 id="1-what-is-this-post-about">1. What Is This Post About?<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#1-what-is-this-post-about"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Notes on Anthropic's guide <a href="https://claude.com/blog/building-an-ai-native-revenue-organization">Building an AI-native revenue organization</a> and the eBook that ships with it.</p>
<p>The title says sales organization, and every example is CRM, pipeline, and call prep. But as you read, you notice that <strong>you can delete the word "sales" and most sentences still hold.</strong> The distance between handing out seats and actually changing an organization, why pilots never reach scale, when you should start reading cost — engineering organizations hit all of the same walls.</p>
<p>The guide's argument in one line:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Hours saved is the floor.</strong> If you build the case for AI on saved time alone, the value <strong>tops out at what you were already paying for those hours.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>If <a href="/en/posts/2026-09-05-frontier-development-teams/">The Tool Was Not the Variable</a> was an <strong>observation</strong> of why 50 teams using the same tool diverged, this guide is closer to an <strong>operating manual</strong> for ending up on the right side of that split. Observations name causes; manuals name order.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="2-same-seat-count-different-outcomes">2. Same Seat Count, Different Outcomes<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#2-same-seat-count-different-outcomes"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 1 opens with a question.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Two revenue organizations equip <strong>the same number of reps with the same access to Claude</strong> and see different returns. What was different?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The answer isn't the tool — it's <strong>what the organization built around it.</strong> The guide names three axes.</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th>Axis</th><th>Question</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Access</strong></td><td><strong>Which systems</strong> can Claude read and write?</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Trust</strong></td><td><strong>How much of the work</strong> is it trusted to finish?</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Sharing</strong></td><td>Are the team's best methods <strong>written down where everyone can run them?</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The maturity ladder moves all three together.</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast"><code data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Hand out a chat assistant     → individual time savings (fast — and most stop here)</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">        ↓ connect systems</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">CRM, call recordings, email   → live data instead of pasted context</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">        ↓ extend trust</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Delegate workflows            → several flows in parallel, not one task</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">        ↓ turn it into an asset</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Plugins, managed agents       → processes run end to end against KPIs/OKRs</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>The line I underlined is <strong>"individual gains show up quickly."</strong> Because they show up quickly, many organizations <strong>read that as success and stop.</strong> A "saved a few hours this week" testimonial arrives in the first month, guaranteed. The problem is that it isn't evidence for climbing to the second rung.</p>
<p>And the guide puts <strong>governance on the same rung, not in an appendix</strong> — who can publish skills, what data each workflow can reach, who owns the results. Exactly the point from <a href="/en/posts/2026-09-06-skills-governance/">Skills Governance</a>: the moment know-how moves into files, it needs an owner's name on it.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="3-the-six-decisions-to-close-before-the-pilot">3. The Six Decisions to Close Before the Pilot<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#3-the-six-decisions-to-close-before-the-pilot"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 2 is the most operational part of the guide: what to decide <strong>before you set a rollout date.</strong></p>








































<table><thead><tr><th>Decision</th><th>What it is</th><th>If you skip it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Define owners</strong></td><td>RevOps is a strong candidate — it owns the CRM and connected systems, plus the pipeline reporting where results get measured</td><td>Adoption becomes nobody's job</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Connect systems</strong></td><td>If reps upload call transcripts by hand, a human is doing a connector's work</td><td>What Claude can finish on its own is capped at paste size</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Bring in IT</strong></td><td>Workspace provisioning, SSO, admin consents for connectors. Agree on provisioning dates <strong>before committing to a pilot date</strong></td><td>Week one of the pilot goes to waiting on accounts</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Bring in security</strong></td><td>The review items are known — <strong>data boundaries, connector permission model, auditability, telemetry export</strong></td><td>The review becomes the pilot's blocker</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Establish success metrics</strong></td><td><strong>One activity metric + one revenue metric</strong>, baseline before deployment, committed readout dates</td><td>You finish with nothing but "it went well"</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Scope spend visibility</strong></td><td>Limits by org, group, and user; role-gated access to costlier capabilities; usage analytics <strong>from day one</strong></td><td>The first invoice becomes the first report</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>There's advice on choosing the pilot cohort too: pick <strong>two or three teams with motivated leads</strong>, not individual volunteers scattered across the org. And provision plugins <strong>at the admin level</strong> — otherwise the pilot splinters into N personal setups.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>What struck me about these six is that <strong>none of them is a decision about AI.</strong> Ownership, integration, security, measurement, budget — standard system-adoption items. AI adoption isn't uniquely hard; it's that <strong>AI looks fine through the first month even when you skip all six,</strong> so you skip them.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h2 id="4-what-actually-changes-in-a-reps-day">4. What Actually Changes in a Rep's Day<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#4-what-actually-changes-in-a-reps-day"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 3 nails the problem down with numbers.</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast"><code data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Where account context lives:  Salesforce · email · call recordings · Slack</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Reassembly time before a call: ~30 minutes</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">The call itself:               15 minutes</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Multiplied by:                 a book of hundreds of accounts</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p><strong>Thirty minutes of prep for a fifteen-minute conversation,</strong> repeated hundreds of times. And those thirty minutes aren't judgment — they're <strong>reassembly.</strong></p>
<p>The guide's answer is Anthropic's Sales plugin, split into commands and skills.</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th>Command</th><th>What it does</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><code>/call-summary</code></td><td>Processes call notes or a transcript, extracts action items, drafts the follow-up, generates an internal summary</td></tr><tr><td><code>/forecast</code></td><td>Builds weighted projections from pipeline data</td></tr><tr><td><code>/pipeline-review</code></td><td>Scores deal health and risk</td></tr></tbody></table>

































<table><thead><tr><th>Skill</th><th>What it does</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><code>account-research</code></td><td>Research a company or person — company intel, key contacts, recent news, hiring signals</td></tr><tr><td><code>call-prep</code></td><td>Prepare for calls — account context, attendee research, suggested agenda, discovery questions</td></tr><tr><td><code>daily-briefing</code></td><td>Prioritized daily briefing — meetings, pipeline alerts, email priorities</td></tr><tr><td><code>draft-outreach</code></td><td>Research-first outreach — research the prospect, then draft personalized email and LinkedIn messages</td></tr><tr><td><code>competitive-intelligence</code></td><td>Competitor research — product comparison, pricing intel, recent releases, differentiation matrix</td></tr><tr><td><code>create-an-asset</code></td><td>Custom sales assets — landing pages, decks, one-pagers, workflow demos</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>At initial setup the plugin <strong>interviews the rep</strong> and commits their name, quota targets, product positioning, and competitor list to a settings file. And the guide adds an honest caveat:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>It works without connecting any of your systems — off web search and whatever the rep has pasted in. But <strong>the plugin only becomes useful once the team connects its tools.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="on-permissions">On permissions<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#on-permissions"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>Connectors <strong>respect each rep's existing permissions</strong> in the underlying systems. Claude can only reach what that rep can already reach. That one sentence explains half of the security review — it isn't a new permission plane, it <strong>inherits the existing one.</strong></p>
<h3 id="and-this-is-where-skills-governance-enters">And this is where skills governance enters<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#and-this-is-where-skills-governance-enters"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>The paragraph that stood out most:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>If one of your top performers writes out his renewal-prep routine or forecast format <strong>as a skill file,</strong> admins can provision it in the team's skill bundle, so new reps <strong>start with working methods on day one.</strong> Skills are editable files, so if your playbook changes, updating the file updates the skill for the whole team.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That's exactly what Cyera's BDRs are doing when they draft outreach with <strong>a skill built from the top performers' best work.</strong> And the guide calls it <strong>a quality improvement as much as a time saving.</strong> I'd argue that distinction is the most important sentence in the whole guide.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="5-the-three-phase-rollout--the-champion-ratio-is-everything">5. The Three-Phase Rollout — The Champion Ratio Is Everything<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#5-the-three-phase-rollout--the-champion-ratio-is-everything"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 4 lays out setup → pilot → scale.</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th>Phase</th><th>What runs in parallel</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Setup</strong></td><td>IT admins: workspace provisioning, SSO / RevOps: first connectors, admin-level plugin provisioning (using access decisions from the security review)</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Pilot</strong></td><td>Champions demo on live accounts, staff office hours, <strong>write the first skills</strong></td></tr><tr><td><strong>Scale</strong></td><td>Pilot-built skills <strong>get promoted to the shared bundle</strong>; the rest of the org is provisioned in waves</td></tr></tbody></table>
<h3 id="the-champion-ratio">The champion ratio<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#the-champion-ratio"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>The one operating rule the guide puts a number on.</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast"><code data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">Two or three champions per department, or one per 25 to 50 users</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">And give them dedicated hours for the work</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>Pick them with your sales managers — <strong>they know which reps already experiment with AI.</strong> And there's a line about the early signal that's genuinely sharp:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>The number of champion-created skills that pilot teams use regularly</strong> is the best early signal of how scaling will go.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Not usage rate. Not satisfaction. <strong>Whether other people keep using what someone else built.</strong> It's a direct measurement of whether individual gains are converting into organizational assets.</p>
<h3 id="waves">Waves<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#waves"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>The expansion pattern is concrete too — <strong>25 seats, then 150, then everyone.</strong> This is also when Claude's outputs move into <strong>standing meetings and shared channels:</strong> <code>/forecast</code> projections show up at the weekly forecast call, managers build QBR decks in Claude, and so on.</p>
<h3 id="from-the-field">From the field<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#from-the-field"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>





















<table><thead><tr><th>Org</th><th>Approach</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Cyera</strong></td><td>A full-day kickoff livestream with <strong>20 department-specific sessions</strong>, followed by <strong>twice-weekly office hours</strong></td></tr><tr><td><strong>Cox</strong></td><td>Seeded champions across teams and scaled through <strong>train-the-trainer</strong></td></tr><tr><td><strong>Cyera (rollout)</strong></td><td>After a brief pilot, <strong>company-wide in 17 days with 40 tools connected.</strong> Beforehand: data-mapping exercises — which systems Claude would connect to, which held production data, how they'd maintain observability</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Seventeen days and forty tools is the impressive part, but what I noted was <strong>"they ran data-mapping exercises beforehand."</strong> The precondition for a fast rollout wasn't fast decisions — it was <strong>homework finished in advance.</strong></p>
<hr>
<h2 id="6-roi--what-to-measure-and-against-what">6. ROI — What to Measure, and Against What<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#6-roi--what-to-measure-and-against-what"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 5 is where the guide's thesis is sharpest.</p>
<h3 id="returns-arrive-in-four-stages">Returns arrive in four stages<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#returns-arrive-in-four-stages"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast"><code data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6">usage  →  output  →  outcomes tracked in the CRM  →  cost</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>The order is the point. Seeing nothing in your outcome metric in week one is normal.</p>
<h3 id="sort-returns-into-three-groups">Sort returns into three groups<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#sort-returns-into-three-groups"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>

























<table><thead><tr><th>Group</th><th>Definition</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Efficiency</strong></td><td>The same work, faster</td><td>Workato: deal prep <strong>3–4 hours → 45 minutes</strong></td></tr><tr><td><strong>Expansion</strong></td><td>More output from the same team</td><td>More accounts covered, more pipeline per rep</td></tr><tr><td><strong>New capabilities</strong></td><td><strong>Work that didn't happen at all before</strong></td><td>Reaching the long tail of accounts no rep had time to touch</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>And in budget conversations, <strong>lead with expansion and new capabilities.</strong> The reasoning is cold:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>If you build the case for AI on hours saved alone, the value <strong>tops out at the cost of those hours,</strong> because the CFO can only value "time freed up" at what the team is paid for that time.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="side-by-side-not-before-and-after">Side-by-side, not before-and-after<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#side-by-side-not-before-and-after"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>The most practical measurement advice in the guide.</p>

















<table><thead><tr><th>The common way</th><th>What the guide proposes</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>The same team <strong>before vs. after</strong></td><td>Pilot group vs. <strong>non-pilot group in the same quarter</strong></td></tr><tr><td>A new quarter changes market and season too</td><td><strong>Same market, same season</strong> — a difference is easier to credit to the program</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Compare pipeline per rep, cycle length, and win rate. While the pilot group sells with Claude and the rest don't, <strong>the CRM is already recording the same metrics for both.</strong> No new instrumentation needed — that's the elegance of the design.</p>
<h3 id="three-signals-that-justify-scaling">Three signals that justify scaling<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#three-signals-that-justify-scaling"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast"><code data-language="markdown" data-theme="github-light-high-contrast github-dark-high-contrast" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#702C00;--shiki-dark:#FFB757">1.</span><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6"> Reps are still producing after the novelty has worn off</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#702C00;--shiki-dark:#FFB757">2.</span><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6"> The quality checks are holding</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#702C00;--shiki-dark:#FFB757">3.</span><span style="--shiki-light:#0E1116;--shiki-dark:#F0F3F6"> Pilot teams are outperforming on the chosen outcome metric (a small gap is fine)</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>The first one is the good one. <strong>"After the novelty has worn off"</strong> is written by someone who knows why early adoption metrics overstate things.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="7-read-spend-in-pairs-not-as-a-leaderboard">7. Read Spend in Pairs, Not as a Leaderboard<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#7-read-spend-in-pairs-not-as-a-leaderboard"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>This sits inside the ROI chapter but deserves its own section.</p>
<p>The first time you pull a spend report, your eye goes to <strong>the reps at the top.</strong> The guide says that alone isn't a reliable metric. Read each rep's spend <strong>next to what that rep produced</strong> — briefs, opportunity updates, proposals.</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>Situation</th><th>Reading</th><th>Action</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>High spend + <strong>producing every day</strong></td><td>The program working as designed. This is why champions got extra headroom</td><td>Leave it alone</td></tr><tr><td>High spend + <strong>little output</strong></td><td>Probably using the tools wrong</td><td><strong>Coach first</strong> — a champion walks them through the commands, skills, and connectors</td></tr><tr><td>Still unchanged after coaching</td><td></td><td>Then lower the cap</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The order — <strong>coaching first, cap reduction second</strong> — is the point. Reversed, you cut people off in the middle of adopting.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="8-the-three-places-adoption-stalls">8. The Three Places Adoption Stalls<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#8-the-three-places-adoption-stalls"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>Chapter 6 covers the patterns where <strong>the pilot succeeds and the org-wide rollout stalls.</strong> All three are caused by something left undecided at setup, not by time.</p>
<h3 id="pitfall-1--a-pilot-with-no-end-date">Pitfall 1 — A pilot with no end date<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#pitfall-1--a-pilot-with-no-end-date"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>The pilot goes well, so the team keeps it going. Nobody set a <strong>date</strong> for the scale decision or named <strong>who makes it.</strong> The sponsoring executive moves on to next quarter's priorities and the rest of the organization never gets provisioned.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>A year later the program is still 25 seats, and its gains are <strong>too small to reach the numbers the CFO reads.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Fix: at setup, <strong>put the scale-decision date on the sponsoring executive's calendar,</strong> name the decision-maker, and <strong>agree in advance on what a "yes" requires.</strong></p>
<h3 id="pitfall-2--scaling-seats-without-scaling-champions">Pitfall 2 — Scaling seats without scaling champions<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#pitfall-2--scaling-seats-without-scaling-champions"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>Two or three champions can support a 25-seat pilot. The mistake is widening to 150 seats with the champion count still at three — office hours stop working, and reps in later waves get <strong>a first week with nobody around to help.</strong></p>
<p>Fix: <strong>hold one champion per 25–50 users through every wave.</strong> Before each wave, the program owner asks the incoming teams' managers to name their champions so current champions can train them.</p>
<h3 id="pitfall-3--ignoring-spend-until-the-first-invoice">Pitfall 3 — Ignoring spend until the first invoice<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#pitfall-3--ignoring-spend-until-the-first-invoice"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>With usage-based billing, monthly cost rises as more reps get provisioned and run more work. Without limits and a regular cadence of reading usage analytics, <strong>the budget owner learns the cost from the invoice.</strong> When it's higher than planned, the common response is to restrict access or remove users — which <strong>interrupts adoption in the middle of the scale phase.</strong></p>
<p>Fix: set limits by org, group, and user <strong>before</strong> the pilot, and read usage analytics <strong>weekly.</strong></p>
<blockquote>
<p>Line the three up and the pattern shows: <strong>the pilot fails because it succeeded.</strong> With a working 25 seats it's comfortable to defer the decision, easier to add people than to add champions, and the cost is still small. All three are <strong>side effects of success.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h2 id="9-translating-it-to-an-engineering-organization">9. Translating It to an Engineering Organization<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#9-translating-it-to-an-engineering-organization"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<p>It's a sales guide, but the substitution is nearly 1:1.</p>





































<table><thead><tr><th>Guide (revenue org)</th><th>Engineering org</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>RevOps as owner</td><td>Platform/DevEx team as owner — it owns CI, repos, observability</td></tr><tr><td>Connectors (Salesforce, Gong, Slack)</td><td>MCP servers, repo access, issue tracker, logs</td></tr><tr><td>One activity + one revenue metric</td><td>One activity metric (PRs opened by agents) + <strong>one outcome metric (lead time, change failure rate)</strong></td></tr><tr><td>Count of champion-created skills</td><td>Count of <strong>someone else's skills/harnesses the team actually reuses</strong></td></tr><tr><td>Pilot vs. non-pilot, same quarter</td><td>Adopting teams vs. non-adopting teams, <strong>same quarter</strong></td></tr><tr><td>Efficiency · expansion · new capabilities</td><td>Same feature faster / more features from the same headcount / <strong>work nobody was touching</strong> (legacy test coverage, long-tail bugs)</td></tr><tr><td>Read the spend leaderboard against output</td><td>Read token usage against <strong>merged changes</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The third category — new capabilities — fits engineering even better. Every repo has a list of <strong>things nobody had time for:</strong> modules with no coverage, warnings nobody reads, the migration everyone is afraid of. Most of the work described in <a href="/en/posts/2026-08-22-delete-and-verify/">Delete, Overshoot, Verify</a> and <a href="/en/posts/2026-08-25-agentic-code-quality/">Quality Lives in the Constraints</a> sits in that column.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="10-easy-things-to-get-wrong">10. Easy Things to Get Wrong<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#10-easy-things-to-get-wrong"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>
<h3 id="misread-1--these-numbers-are-a-reproducible-expectation">Misread 1 — "These numbers are a reproducible expectation"<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#misread-1--these-numbers-are-a-reproducible-expectation"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>Cox's 7x, Cyera's 88%, one rep's 90 minutes a day. Impressive, but these are <strong>vendor-published customer stories.</strong> Methodology, duration, and baselines aren't disclosed, and only the successes get written up. They're <strong>existence proofs,</strong> not <strong>expected values.</strong> Notably, the guide's own advice isn't "cite these numbers" — it's <strong>"compare two groups in your own org in the same quarter."</strong> Following that is a better use of the guide than believing the numbers.</p>
<h3 id="misread-2--wiring-up-connectors-is-technical-work">Misread 2 — "Wiring up connectors is technical work"<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#misread-2--wiring-up-connectors-is-technical-work"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>It's not an accident that Chapter 2 groups connectors <strong>with security and IT.</strong> A connector changes the access surface, and access is the first axis of the maturity ladder. <strong>Deciding what to connect is deciding how far Claude can finish work</strong> — that's an organizational decision, not an engineering ticket.</p>
<h3 id="misread-3--sharing-skills-spreads-best-practice">Misread 3 — "Sharing skills spreads best practice"<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#misread-3--sharing-skills-spreads-best-practice"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>It does spread. But everything from <a href="/en/posts/2026-09-06-skills-governance/">Skills Governance</a> comes along with it — ownerless skills, copies, instructions that quietly rot when the model changes. This guide describes the <strong>path</strong> from a champion's skill to the team bundle, but <strong>who maintains that bundle</strong> gets one sentence. In practice that sentence is next quarter's entire workload.</p>
<h3 id="misread-4--a-longer-pilot-accumulates-evidence">Misread 4 — "A longer pilot accumulates evidence"<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#misread-4--a-longer-pilot-accumulates-evidence"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h3>
<p>Pitfall 1 refutes exactly this intuition. What a long pilot accumulates isn't evidence — it's <strong>the absence of a decision.</strong> The guide doesn't ask for a longer pilot; it asks you to <strong>write down what a "yes" requires, in advance.</strong></p>
<hr>
<h2 id="11-wrapping-up">11. Wrapping Up<a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#11-wrapping-up"><span class="icon icon-link"></span></a></h2>


















































<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>Point</th><th>Why</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>1. Seat count isn't the variable</strong></td><td>Access, trust, and sharing decide the outcome</td><td>The same rep count produces different returns</td></tr><tr><td><strong>2. Close six things at setup</strong></td><td>Owner, connections, IT, security, metrics, spend</td><td>AI looks fine through month one even when you skip them</td></tr><tr><td><strong>3. The best early signal isn't usage</strong></td><td><strong>How many champion-built skills the team keeps using</strong></td><td>It directly measures individual gains becoming org assets</td></tr><tr><td><strong>4. Hold 25–50:1 champions every wave</strong></td><td>Adding seats alone abandons the later waves</td><td>Scaling is a support-staffing problem, not a seat problem</td></tr><tr><td><strong>5. Side-by-side beats before-and-after</strong></td><td>Two groups, same quarter, same market</td><td>Differences are easier to credit to the program</td></tr><tr><td><strong>6. Lead with expansion and new capabilities</strong></td><td>Hours saved tops out at payroll</td><td>The case that survives the CFO conversation</td></tr><tr><td><strong>7. Read spend paired with output</strong></td><td>Coach first, lower caps second</td><td>A leaderboard alone cuts people off mid-adoption</td></tr><tr><td><strong>8. The pilot fails because it succeeded</strong></td><td>End date, champion ratio, spend limits</td><td>All three are side effects of success</td></tr></tbody></table>

<ul>
<li><a href="/en/posts/2026-08-23-ai-native-sdlc-playbook/">The AI-Native SDLC Playbook</a> — <strong>how to redesign the process</strong></li>

<li><a href="/en/posts/2026-09-06-skills-governance/">Skills Governance</a> — <strong>who owns know-how once it becomes a file</strong></li>
<li>This post — <strong>in what order to run that transition, and what to prove it with</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>The sentence that stayed with me longest was the last one in the ROI chapter: build the case on saved time and the value stops at payroll. That isn't a sales-only problem. It's also why <strong>"AI made our development faster"</strong> carries so little weight in next year's budget conversation. If you only report what got faster, <strong>the part about work you never used to do at all</strong> falls out of the report.</p>
